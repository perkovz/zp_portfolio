When I began working on the Cleveland Clinic Design System, I faced several significant challenges. The design and development teams operated in silos, with minimal collaboration between them. There was little appetite or recognition from business leaders about the importance of having a unified design system. Additionally, I quickly discovered that some contributors were territorial about their responsibilities and didn't appreciate when I stepped outside my perceived lane to solve problems.

---

## Getting the Support I Needed First

Before tackling these siloed teams and lack of leadership buy-in, I made sure to get backing from my manager and peers at Cleveland Clinic. This proved invaluable in a department where the value of a design system wasn't yet recognized. The support from my boss provided me with credibility, freedom (to an extent), and valuable feedback, while forcing me to collect my thoughts about how to bridge these organizational divides.

I had three main reasons for talking with my manager before going the extra mile:
- To ensure my thoughts aligned with Cleveland Clinic's greater vision and plans, despite the lack of formal support for a design system
- To get approval before doing any major work that might cross into others' territories
- To gain practical advice from someone who could help me navigate the organizational resistance

---

## Making Stakeholders Engaged by Adding Value

With the design and development teams operating separately, I knew I needed to move quickly from idea generation to actual implementation that would benefit both sides. In my official role of Frontend developer I had a unique position and my strategy for breaking down these silos consisted of:

1. Defining the key players from both design and development teams - identifying the people I needed to bring together to make the design system work
2. Including them early, soliciting their feedback, and making them part of the solution to overcome the departmental divisions
3. Doing work that solved their existing problems with the fragmented approach to design

I looked for quick victories that would demonstrate value to skeptical business leaders. These had to be elements of the design system that addressed pain points caused by the siloed structure. I put extra time into building working solutions that could serve as tangible proof of what a unified system could achieve.

I discovered that some people were particularly protective of their domains when I attempted to solve cross-functional problems. This territorial reaction was stronger than I expected. Fortunately, by focusing on solutions that made everyone's jobs easier, I was able to gradually overcome this resistance. This reinforced my decision to build alliances with my boss and key stakeholders early in the process.

To make critical stakeholders interested, I:
1. Chose tools that would solve specific problems at the intersection of design and development
2. Started the work but deliberately left room for others to contribute, helping them feel ownership over the system
3. Made my solutions so compelling that even the most territorial colleagues could see how they benefited from collaboration

---

## Promoting the Design System Within the Team

Once I had convinced a few key stakeholders from both sides of the design-development divide, I used my marketing skills to promote our solution to the broader team and to business leaders who hadn't yet recognized the value of a design system.

This promotion phase was essential in making our new approach official and getting more people to cross departmental boundaries. When business leaders began to see concrete improvements in efficiency and consistency, they became more supportive of the initiative.


## Learning to Let Go

As the system gained traction, I faced a new challenge - knowing when to step back. After putting so much effort into crossing organizational boundaries and convincing skeptical stakeholders, I had developed a strong sense of ownership over the project. I asked myself: "Can I walk away from the design system I fought so hard to establish?"

To manage this inner conflict, I focused on:
1. **Empathy** - recognizing that some of the territorial behavior I had encountered in others was now appearing in myself
2. **Creating new mental models that celebrated when former skeptics took ownership**: "When Nikki from design and John from development collaborate on the component library without my intervention, that's a win."

When I learned to let go, I wasn't abandoning the system but rather allowing it to become truly integrated into the organization rather than being seen as "my project."

---

## Keeping the Design System Growing

I learned that without continuous attention, the teams could easily slip back into their siloed ways of working. The same happens with design systems - they get outdated, fall out of use, and eventually die, especially when organizational support is fragile.

To prevent this from happening, I kept track of cross-functional collaborations and stepped in when I noticed old patterns reemerging. To avoid being seen as overstepping my boundaries again, I developed a rhythm: "Stepping out > Using and observing > Stepping back in > Fixing and improving > Stepping back out > Repeat."

Since this design system improvement initiative came from within, and initially lacked strong business backing, I recognized there was a possibility our progress could stall when facing other priorities. Since I expected that to happen, I was ready to advocate for the system's importance when needed.

The Cleveland Clinic Design System taught me the importance of not just improving technical processes, but also addressing organizational challenges like siloed teams, territorial ownership, and lack of leadership buy-in. Breaking down these barriers proved just as important as the design work itself.
